<p>Every clinic runs two workloads. One is visible: patients in the waiting area, consultations, prescriptions. The other rarely appears on any report: the phone that keeps ringing, notes finished after closing time, old reports dug out of paper files and chat threads, and follow-up calls that never happen because the day ran out.</p>

<p><strong>A note on the evidence:</strong> Much of the strongest recent data comes from the US and the UK, often from large health systems rather than independent clinics. We say where each finding comes from. Examples marked "hypothetical" are illustrations, not data.</p>

<h2>What Is the Hidden Cost of Clinic Administration?</h2>

<p>The hidden cost is time: doctors' evenings, front-desk hours and appointment slots lost to tasks that don't need clinical judgement. Because it is spread across dozens of small jobs, it rarely shows up as a single line in the accounts.</p>

<p>In August 2025, the American Medical Association reported 2024 data from nearly 18,000 physician responses, collected through organisations taking part in its Organizational Biopsy. The average workweek was 57.8 hours, and more than one in five physicians (22.5%) reported more than eight hours of EHR work outside normal hours, up from 20.9% the year before. The share reporting at least one burnout symptom fell to 43.2%, from 53% in 2022, but the after-hours work has not gone away.</p>

<p>Clinic-level data points the same way. A January 2024 study in the Annals of Family Medicine followed 141 primary care physicians at one US academic health centre from 2019 to 2023. Their EHR time per eight hours of scheduled appointments rose by 28.4 minutes (7.8%), to roughly 391 minutes.</p>

<p>Comparable data for private clinics in India is scarce, so treat these figures as directional.</p>

<h2>Four Admin Tasks That Drain Clinic Efficiency and Patient Experience</h2>

<p>Four admin tasks affect both how efficiently a clinic runs and how patients experience it: booking calls, clinical documentation, fragmented patient records and manual follow-ups.</p>

<h3>Booking calls and the front-desk bottleneck</h3>

<p>Getting through by phone remains a common frustration. England's GP Patient Survey 2026 (654,714 responses, published July 2026) found that 56.9% of patients said it was easy to contact their practice by phone. That is up from 52.9% in 2025, but it still means about four in ten did not describe phone contact as easy. Meanwhile, the share whose last contact was online rose to 30.8%, from 22.4%.</p>

<p><em>Hypothetical example:</em> if a receptionist handles 60 booking-related calls a day at three minutes each, that is three hours of staff time, before counting missed calls, callbacks and patients waiting at the counter. Your numbers will differ; the point is that this time is worth measuring.</p>

<h3>Clinical documentation that follows doctors home</h3>

<p>Notes, prescriptions, referral letters and care instructions are essential. When they are typed after each visit, or after the last patient leaves, they compete with attention during the consultation and with rest after it. As the AMA figures show, this work spills well beyond working hours for a sizeable minority of doctors. Documentation burden is also widely linked to physician burnout.</p>

<h3>Fragmented patient records</h3>

<p>When a patient's history is split between paper files, lab PDFs, phone galleries and separate software, the cost shows up as repeated questions, repeated tests and slower decisions. US federal data (ONC, August 2025) shows that in 2024, 10% of people who had seen a provider in the past year had to redo a test because earlier results were unavailable, up from 6% in 2020.</p>

<p>India is building national infrastructure for this. As of 17 September 2026, more than 97.61 crore ABHA IDs were operational and more than 119.95 crore health records had been linked (PIB, September 2026). But a national ID does not by itself join up a clinic's own patient records.</p>

<h3>Manual follow-ups and patient no-shows</h3>

<p>A missed appointment leaves an empty slot. A missed follow-up can leave a gap in care. An August 2026 MGMA Stat poll of 190 US medical groups found that 32% had higher no-show rates so far in 2026 than in 2025, 58% about the same and 10% lower. Leaders at groups with rising rates said they were already using text and phone reminders, confirmation calls and other outreach.</p>

<p>Reminders help, but modestly. A 2026 systematic review of hospital outpatient studies found that reminders improved attendance compared with usual care (risk ratio 1.11; 10 studies, 8,236 participants). For SMS reminders alone, the estimate was positive but not statistically significant. Because this is hospital evidence, clinic results may differ. The lesson for clinics: one generic reminder is a starting point, not a strategy for reducing patient no-shows.</p>

<h3>AI medical scribes and clinical documentation</h3>

<p>The evidence on AI medical scribes has matured quickly, with important caveats:</p>

<ul>

<li><strong>Randomised trial (UCLA Health, NEJM AI, November 2025):</strong> 238 physicians across 14 specialties at one academic medical centre. With one of the two tools tested, time per note fell from 4 min 30 s to 3 min 49 s, compared with an 18-second fall under usual care. The other tool's difference was not statistically significant. Both showed modest improvements in burnout scores.</li>

<li><strong>Pre-post study (JAMA Network Open, October 2025):</strong> ambulatory clinicians at six US health systems used one ambient scribe. Self-reported burnout fell from 51.9% to 38.8% after 30 days. The study had no control group.</li>

<li><strong>Large rollout (Kaiser Permanente, reported April 2025):</strong> 7,260 physicians used an AI scribe in more than 2.5 million encounters between October 2023 and December 2024. An analysis estimated that it saved nearly 16,000 hours of documentation time.</li>

</ul>

<p>All three come from large US health systems, and one product's results do not automatically transfer to another. Still, they point the same way: less time on notes, less mental load and more attention on the patient.</p>

<h2>Why Doctor Oversight Matters When Using AI in Healthcare</h2>

<p>Doctor oversight matters because AI drafts still contain errors, and the clinician signs the record.</p>

<p>A September 2025 study in BMJ Digital Health &amp; AI tested seven commercial AI scribes on eight simulated primary care consultations in England. None produced error-free summaries. Omissions made up 83.8% of errors, and hallucinations were rarer but more often clinically serious. The authors advised clinicians to check medications and psychosocial details in particular. In the UCLA trial, physicians reported occasional clinically significant inaccuracies and one mild patient safety event. As the trial's senior author put it, the technology "requires active physician oversight, not passive acceptance."</p>

<p>Doctors want that role. In the AMA's March 2026 survey of nearly 1,700 physicians, 81% said they use AI in their practice, up from 38% in 2023. Most (85%) wanted to be consulted on, or directly involved in, decisions about AI adoption. In India, ICMR's 2023 ethical guidelines for AI in healthcare cover governance of AI use, ethics review and informed consent.</p>

<p>Practical oversight rules:</p>

<ul>

<li>The doctor reviews and signs every AI-drafted note and prescription.</li>

<li>Check the high-risk fields every time: medicines, doses, allergies, plan and follow-up date.</li>

<li>In the first months, audit a small sample of AI-drafted notes each week.</li>

<li>Keep a record of what the AI suggested and what was accepted.</li>

<li>Tell patients when AI is used in their consultation.</li>

</ul>

<p>Our first AI governance rule at Medrita is simple: "AI assists. A human decides." Every AI suggestion appears in the audit log, and clinics can switch AI features off per feature and per branch.</p>

<h2>What Are the Challenges of Implementing AI in a Clinic?</h2>

<p>The main challenges are data protection, fit with real consultations, change management and over-automation.</p>

<p><strong>Data protection:</strong> India's DPDP Rules were notified in November 2025 with an 18-month phased compliance timeline. They require clear consent notices and plain-language notice to affected individuals after a data breach. Ask any vendor, including us, where patient data is stored, which subprocessors handle it and what its compliance status is. We publish ours on our <a href="https://medrita.com/trust">Trust page</a>, including data residency, our subprocessor list and which certifications are live, in progress or on the roadmap.</p>

<p><strong>Fit with real consultations:</strong> The scribe studies cited here come from US and UK settings. Before relying on any tool, test it on your own consultations, languages and accents.</p>

<p><strong>Change management:</strong> Staff need training time and a named person to raise problems with. Expect to adjust templates and settings in the first weeks.</p>

<p><strong>Over-automation:</strong> Use clinic-approved templates for automated messages. A wrong care instruction sent automatically is still the clinic's responsibility.</p>

<h2>How to Measure Results from AI Clinic Management Software</h2>

<p>Measure results against your own baseline, not a vendor's promised savings, including ours. Track:</p>

<ul>

<li><strong>Documentation:</strong> minutes to complete a note, notes open at day end, after-hours documentation time.</li>

<li><strong>Access:</strong> online versus phone bookings, missed calls, days to the next available appointment.</li>

<li><strong>Attendance:</strong> no-show rate by doctor and visit type; cancelled slots refilled.</li>

<li><strong>Follow-up:</strong> patients with a follow-up date who returned on time.</li>

<li><strong>Records:</strong> duplicate records; time taken to find previous reports.</li>

<li><strong>Safety and quality:</strong> AI drafts needing substantive edits; errors found in audits.</li>

<li><strong>People:</strong> a short monthly burnout check-in for doctors and staff.</li>

</ul>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions About AI in Clinic Management</h2>

<h3>Are AI medical scribes accurate?</h3>

<p>Useful, but not error-free. In the studies above, omissions were the most common error, so every AI-drafted note needs a doctor's review before it is signed.</p>

<h3>Will patients accept AI during their consultation?</h3>

<p>Early evidence suggests most will. In the UCLA trial, fewer than 10% of patients declined the AI scribe. In a Kaiser Permanente survey of 118 patients, two-thirds said they were comfortable with it and 8% reported some discomfort. Tell patients when AI is used and respect their choice.</p>

<h3>Can AI replace a clinic receptionist?</h3>

<p>No. AI can take routine bookings, reminders and record look-ups off the front desk, but patients who are anxious, unwell or confused still need a person. The aim is to give reception more time for those conversations.</p>

<h2>The Bottom Line</h2>

<p>Admin work will never disappear from medicine, and it shouldn't: good records and good follow-up are part of good care. The goal is to stop paying for that work with doctors' evenings and patients' patience. With clear oversight and honest measurement, AI clinic management software can take on the repetitive parts, so clinicians can spend their time where they are needed most.</p>
